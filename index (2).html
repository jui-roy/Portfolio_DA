Case study: Where does Superstore make and lose money?

Business question: sales grew every year after 2015, but is the business actually profitable, and which products and discount levels leak profit? Filter by year and region; the charts and findings update.

DataSample Superstore (Tableau): 9,994 order lines, 5,009 orders, 793 customers, Jan 2014 to Dec 2017. No missing values.
MethodLoaded in Python (pandas), grouped by year, region, sub-category and discount band, then compared margins (profit divided by sales).
CaveatThe Returns sheet lists 296 returned orders (about 7.9% of sales value). Figures here are before returns.

Interview talking points

What was the headline insight?

Overall margin is 12.5% on $2.30M of sales, but order lines discounted over 40% (933 of 9,994) run at a margin of about minus 77%. Heavy discounting is the single biggest profit leak.

Which products lose money?

Tables (about minus $17.7K), Bookcases (minus $3.5K) and Supplies (minus $1.2K) are loss-making over four years, even though Tables and Bookcases bring in real revenue. Checking their discount levels is the first step.

How did you approach it?

Checked data quality, split results by time, region, product and discount band, then used margin rather than raw profit so large and small segments compare fairly. Each chart answers one question.

What would you do next?

Cap discounts around 20% on furniture, test it on one region, and measure margin and volume for a quarter. I would also net returns out of sales and add customer-level analysis.

What are the limits of this analysis?

Discount is correlated with loss here, but this is observational data, so it does not prove discounts alone cause the loss. Product cost and promotion reasons are missing.

Case study: Why do telecom customers leave?

Business question: about one in four customers cancels. Who is most at risk, and how much monthly revenue walks out the door? Filter by contract and internet service to see how the picture changes.

DataTelco Customer Churn (IBM sample): 7,043 customers, 21 columns, one snapshot of a telecom provider.
MethodCleaned in Python (pandas), grouped customers by contract, internet service, tenure band, payment method and support, then compared churn rates.
Cleaning noteTotalCharges is blank for 11 customers, all with 0 months of tenure (brand new). I left them out of any charge totals.

Interview talking points

What was the headline insight?

Overall churn is 26.5% (1,869 of 7,043 customers), but month-to-month customers churn at 42.7% against 2.8% on two-year contracts. Contract type separates risk better than anything else.

Where is the revenue risk?

Churned customers paid about $74 a month on average versus $61 for those who stayed. They account for roughly $139K of $456K in monthly charges, about 30%.

What surprised you?

Electronic check users churn at 45.3%, versus 15 to 17% on automatic payments. Fiber optic customers churn at 41.9% versus 19.0% on DSL, which may point to price or service quality.

What would you recommend?

Offer first-year customers on month-to-month plans a discount to switch to an annual contract with autopay, and bundle tech support, since customers with it churn at 15.2% versus 41.6% without.

What are the limits?

Factors overlap: new customers are more often on month-to-month plans, so tenure and contract are tangled together. This shows association, not proof of cause, and a controlled test would be the next step.

2. Netflix catalogue: cleaning and analysis

Question: how did Netflix’s catalogue grow, and what is in it? Data: public Netflix titles file, 6,234 titles, with messy text columns cleaned using formulas only.

How did you clean the data?

Year added comes from the last four characters of the trimmed date text, primary country and genre from the text before the first comma, and movie minutes from the number before the space in duration. Each step is a visible formula, and a cleaning log counts the blanks.
